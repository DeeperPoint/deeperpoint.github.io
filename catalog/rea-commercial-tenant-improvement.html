Commercial Tenant Improvement: Trade Assembly for Lease-Closing Buildouts

The Thin Market Problem

Commercial lease negotiations for office and retail space increasingly include landlord-provided tenant improvement (TI) allowances that cover base buildout costs. A prospective tenant who is deciding between two available spaces will award the lease to the landlord whose space comes with a credible, detailed, costed TI plan presented at the negotiation stage—ideally with contractor commitments, not just estimates. Property managers at mid-size commercial properties do not have standing TI contractor teams assembled and on call. They manage maintenance with a small roster of general maintenance trades, but specialized TI requirements—structured IT cabling and patch panel installation, HVAC zoning reconfiguration to match the new tenant's open-plan layout, custom reception millwork, accessibility washroom retrofits to current AODA standards, and illuminated exterior signage—require specialty contractor relationships that the property manager does not maintain. The landlord who can say 'our contractor team has committed to an eight-week build timeline starting at lease signing' closes leases faster than the landlord who says 'we'd solicit three quotes after you sign.'

Dominant Forces
  • Offering complexity — a complete TI package for a professional office requires structural, mechanical (HVAC), electrical, IT infrastructure, millwork, accessibility compliance, and signage trades, each with specific commercial tenancy experience
  • Timeline commitment — what closes leases is not the estimate but the committed timeline; specialty trades must be able to confirm start dates before the lease is signed, not after
  • Vacancy cost pressure — every month of vacancy on a commercial space costs the landlord net operating income; slow TI assembly extends vacancy timelines and directly reduces property value
  • Tenant industry specificity — a law firm's TI requirements (privacy offices, secure server room, reception standards) are structurally different from a tech company's (open plan, server access, EV charging in parking); the TI team must have experience in the prospective tenant's space type
  • Opacity — property managers cannot efficiently determine which specialty trade contractors have current availability and commercial tenancy experience for the specific space type and timeline
How DeeperPoint Helps

Semantic matching encodes contractor profiles (trade specialization, commercial TI experience by tenant type, availability for project start dates, timeline commitment track record, typical scope size in square footage) against property manager opportunity signals (space type, square footage, prospective tenant industry, TI scope components required, target timeline, budget). Pre-built TI contractor panels—assembled in advance for a property or geographic market—allow property managers to respond to lease inquiries with committed contractor timelines, not speculative estimates.

Economic Upside

A commercial space leasing at $28/sq.ft. on a 4,000 sq.ft. floor generates $112,000/year in rent. Every month of additional vacancy while the TI team is being assembled costs the landlord approximately $9,300. A TI contractor panel that enables lease closing one month faster than the competition pays for itself immediately. At scale, a property manager with a pre-assembled TI trade panel can quote committed TI timelines across their entire portfolio, converting leasing from a reactive negotiation into a proactive competitive differentiator.

Narrative Story
The Eight-Week Promise

Characters: Carla — property manager, 65,000 sq.ft. mixed suburban office park, Oakville; three vacant floors over 14 months, Dev — owner, commercial IT cabling and structured wiring company, Mississauga; commercial office buildout specialist

Act A — The Fourteen-Month Vacancy

Carla manages a three-building suburban office park in Oakville. Two of her three buildings are at 94% occupancy. The third building's second floor — 8,200 sq.ft. — has been vacant for fourteen months. She has shown it seventeen times. She has lost four prospective tenants who chose other buildings.

The pattern is consistent: the prospective tenants like the space, they like the building, they like the location. They ask about tenant improvements — specifically about timeline from lease signing to occupancy. When Carla says "we'd engage contractors after lease signing and target ten to fourteen weeks," they thank her and lease the space two buildings over whose landlord committed to eight weeks.

Eight weeks is the competitive threshold in Oakville's suburban office market right now. Carla knows this. What she doesn't know is how to commit to eight weeks without having her TI contractor panel assembled in advance.

Act B — The Story

The tech company inquiry was her best prospect in six months. Forty people, five-year term at $26/sq.ft. The director of operations sent a requirements document: open plan with 12 private offices, server room with dedicated cooling, structured cabling for 50 workstations, HVAC reconfiguration for the open-plan layout, reception desk with custom millwork, AODA-compliant accessible washroom upgrade, and exterior signage for the building directory.

Carla knew she could do all of it. She didn't know how fast.

She called her general maintenance contractor first. He did drywall and tile but not structured cabling or custom millwork. He gave her two referrals for IT cabling—one who had a residential background, one who specialized in commercial. She called both. One was available, one was booked for six weeks on another project.

The HVAC reconfiguration required a licensed mechanical contractor with commercial zoning and TSSA credentials. Her maintenance HVAC contact did residential repairs; he referred her out. Three calls. One available.

Dev's commercial IT cabling company had completed 23 office TI projects in the Mississauga-Oakville corridor. He had done structured cabling for law firms, medical offices, and three tech companies. His team could commit to a 10-business-day cabling installation for an 8,200 sq.ft. floor. He had capacity available.

He was not in Carla's network. He had registered on the MarketForge commercial TI platform six weeks earlier, tagging his profile with "tech company office buildout," "structured cabling," "Oakville/Mississauga," and "available Q2."

Carla found him through the platform in the same week she lost the tech company inquiry to the building two blocks over. The building two blocks over had Dev's equivalent already on their pre-assembled TI panel.

She assembled her TI trade panel over the following three weeks. The next tech company inquiry she received, she committed to eight weeks at the negotiation. She closed the lease.

Act C — Why This Market Stays Broken Without Infrastructure

Missing one trade—IT cabling—prevented Carla from committing to the timeline that would have closed a five-year lease. The cost of that missed lease commitment: fourteen more months of vacancy on 8,200 sq.ft. at $26/sq.ft., net approximately $177,000 in forgone revenue on that floor alone.

Dev was available, qualified, and less than 15 kilometres from the property. He was invisible because there was no mechanism by which Carla's trade search reached him before she started cold-calling referral chains.

Thin market infrastructure makes the commercially-specialized, availability-confirmed trade visible at the moment the lease negotiation creates the assembly need—converting the property manager's speculative "we'd engage contractors after signing" into a committed timeline that closes deals.

Characters are fictional. Commercial TI timelines in Ontario's suburban office market, AODA accessibility requirements, and TSSA mechanical contractor credentials are real. DeeperPoint is building the infrastructure this story describes.
