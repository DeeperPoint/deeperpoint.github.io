The Thin Market Problem

Federal impact assessment panels require specialists whose expertise is precisely calibrated to the specific ecological, technical, and Indigenous rights dimensions of each project under review. Rosters of available, conflict-checked reviewers in areas like boreal peatland hydrology, marine acoustic ecology, or Northern First Nations traditional land use methodologies are not centrally maintained in any searchable form. The Impact Assessment Agency of Canada assembles panels through personal networks, provincial referrals, and academic directories — a process that favours well-connected institutions in southern Ontario and British Columbia and systematically underrepresents practitioners in affected regions with direct fieldwork experience. For novel project categories — a lithium brine extraction operation, a deep sea cable corridor, a hydrogen pipeline through permafrost — no prior panel composition serves as a template.

Dominant Forces
  • Participant scarcity — the pool of credentialed, conflict-checked specialists in narrow environmental assessment subfields is genuinely small nationally
  • Opacity — no searchable directory links specialist expertise profiles to the specific project categories, biomes, and Indigenous territory dimensions where that expertise applies
  • Offering complexity — each panel seat requires matching on disciplinary expertise, geographic fieldwork history, conflict-of-interest status relative to the specific proponent, and availability within the statutory timeline
  • Regulatory time pressure — the Impact Assessment Act specifies statutory review timelines; delays in panel assembly compress substantive review time
  • Geographic and institutional concentration — academic referral networks systematically favour major research universities, leaving fieldwork practitioners and northern-based experts invisible to panel coordinators
How DeeperPoint Helps

Semantic matching encodes specialist profiles (disciplinary expertise, biome-specific fieldwork history, Indigenous territory and rights expertise, conflict-of-interest declarations relative to major proponents) against panel seat requirements (project category, geographic footprint, statutory expertise mandates, available review timeline). KnowledgeSlot curates the Impact Assessment Act specialist mandate requirements, conflict-of-interest rules under the Lobbyists Registration Act, and biome-specific ecological baseline methodologies. The conflict-check layer automates screening against the proponent registry before surfacing candidates to coordinators.

Economic Upside

Every month of delay in federal impact assessment review costs proponents hundreds of thousands of dollars in carrying costs and delays royalty revenue to affected provinces. Better specialist matching reduces panel assembly time, increases the depth of relevant expertise on review panels, and reduces the litigation risk that arises when impacted parties can demonstrate that the panel lacked the specific expertise the statute requires.

Narrative Story
The Peatland Nobody Knew

Characters: Dr. Yolanda — boreal peatland ecohydrologist, Prince George, BC; no prior federal panel appointments, Marcus — senior panel coordinator, Impact Assessment Agency of Canada, Ottawa

✎ This story is in draft.

Act A — The Narrow Pool

The Impact Assessment Act requires federal review panels to include specialists whose expertise is specifically relevant to the ecological systems within a project's footprint. For most project categories — a hydroelectric dam, a metals mine, a highway corridor — a decade of panel precedent provides a template. Coordinators know which disciplines are required, and they know which practitioners have served before.

For novel project types in less-studied ecosystems, that precedent does not exist. The coordinator must determine, from first principles, which specialisms are mandatory and then find credentialed, available, conflict-free practitioners in fields where the national pool may number in the dozens.

The following is a short fictional account of what happens when one of those fields has a discovery problem.

Act B — The Story

Marcus is assembling the specialist panel for a proposed bitumen extraction and pipeline corridor in the Peace-Athabasca drainage basin — a project category that intersects boreal peatland ecology, freshwater hydrology, and caribou corridor management in ways no prior panel has addressed combinatorially.

His statutory framework requires, among other seats, a peatland ecohydrologist whose fieldwork experience is specific to boreal systems — not temperate bogs, not subarctic tundra, but the boreal peatland complex of the northern interior. He queries his agency's internal roster. Three names. One has a conflict: their university received proponent funding. One is unavailable for the statutory window. One has retired from fieldwork.

His next step is the academic directory route. He emails the forestry faculty chairs at four major Canadian universities. Two weeks pass.

Dr. Yolanda completed her doctorate at the University of Northern British Columbia on boreal peatland water table dynamics under climate forcing. She has spent eight field seasons in the Peace-Athabasca watershed. She has published twelve peer-reviewed papers, testified at a BC Environmental Assessment Office hearing, and consulted for two First Nations governments on cumulative effects. She has never received a federal panel appointment inquiry.

She is not in Marcus's agency roster. She is not on the radar of any of the four faculty chairs Marcus emailed — she is not at their institutions, and she did not train under their networks.

She had registered on the MarketForge government specialist matching platform eighteen months earlier, prompted by a BC Ministry of Environment circular. Her profile includes: disciplinary expertise, specific geographic fieldwork zones (Peace-Athabasca, Liard Basin, northern interior BC), First Nations consultation experience, conflict-of-interest declaration structure, availability calendar, and statutory review experience.

Marcus's panel coordinator account on the platform had been provisioned by his agency three months prior. He had not used it for a panel assembly before.

He entered the panel seat requirements: boreal peatland ecohydrology, Peace-Athabasca fieldwork experience preferred, no prior proponent relationship with the named companies, available for the statutory review window starting in six weeks.

The platform returned two profiles. Dr. Yolanda was one of them. Her conflict-of-interest pre-screening against the proponent's corporate registry returned clear.

Marcus sent an outreach request through the platform at 11 AM. Dr. Yolanda accepted the panel invitation by 3 PM the same day.

The panel assembled in full within eleven working days — three weeks ahead of the statutory deadline pressure point that had historically caused the agency to compress expert testimony time.

The panel's peatland ecohydrology chapter was later cited by the Federal Court as demonstrating the expertise standard the Act requires.

Act C — Why This Market Stays Broken Without Infrastructure

Dr. Yolanda's profile, fieldwork history, and availability were all real and accessible — to anyone who knew to look for her. The problem is that federal panel coordinators search through the networks they already have, and the networks they already have are built from prior panels, academic affiliations, and institutional referrals that systematically reproduce the same geography and institutional home.

The specialist pool for narrow environmental subdisciplines is not large enough to tolerate that inefficiency. When the pool is forty people and discovery mechanisms reach twenty of them, the twenty who are not reachable are not randomly distributed — they are concentrated in smaller institutions, in northern communities, and in practitioner rather than academic career tracks.

Thin market infrastructure does not expand the pool. It makes the whole pool searchable — including the practitioners who have never been appointedand never will be, not because their expertise is insufficient but because their name has never appeared in the right person's inbox.

Characters are fictional. The Impact Assessment Act specialist requirements, conflict-of-interest screening obligations, and Peace-Athabasca ecological baseline documentation requirements are real. DeeperPoint is building the infrastructure this story describes.
