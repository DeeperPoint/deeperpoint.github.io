The Thin Market Problem

The contract machining market has two chronic failures. On the supply side, specialty machining shops — gun drilling, Swiss-turn, EDM, precision grinding, ultra-precision diamond turning — cannot effectively market their capabilities to a buyer universe that doesn't know what to search for. On the demand side, OEM procurement and manufacturing engineers spend days or weeks cold-calling shops to determine whether any has the right combination of equipment, certifications, and available capacity for a specific job. General outsourcing platforms match only by process category; they cannot determine whether a shop's gundrilling capacity can achieve the L/D ratio, surface finish, and repeatability a specific hydraulic manifold requires. The transaction cost of discovering whether a match exists is so high that it often exceeds the value of the job itself for small-volume specialty work.

Dominant Forces
  • Opacity — specialty capability (specific machine configurations, demonstrated tolerances, material experience) is locked in shop-floor knowledge invisible to any external directory
  • Search friction — buyers cannot specify their need precisely enough for keyword search to find a relevant match; the vocabulary of machining capability is too technical
  • Information asymmetry — buyers cannot evaluate shop capability from a website; sellers cannot know that their specific capability is exactly what a buyer needs
  • Deal complexity — quoting specialty machining requires material specs, tolerance requirements, surface finish, quantity, and delivery time — a structured conversation, not a catalog transaction
  • Cold-start fragmentation — specialty capability is geographically dispersed; no single region has enough specialty shops in any one category to sustain a conventional marketplace
How DeeperPoint Helps

KnowledgeSlot holds the machining capability taxonomy — process types, machine configurations, material grades, achievable tolerance ranges, surface finish standards — enabling semantic matching between a buyer's engineered requirement (expressed in drawing language or natural text) and a shop's documented capability profile. The Cosolvent matching layer handles the translation between buyer intent (expressed as a part requirement) and seller capability (expressed as process terms). The Generative Match Story produces a capability alignment brief — explaining to the buyer why a specific shop's equipment and experience match their requirement, and explaining to the shop what the job involves before either party invests in a full quote.

Economic Upside

Canada's custom machining sector generates an estimated $8–12B annually. A matching platform that reduces buyer search time from days to hours — and improves shop utilization by routing specialty work to the shops best qualified to do it — could capture 1–2% of transaction value in facilitation fees, generating $80–$240M in annual platform revenue while materially improving capital efficiency across the sector.

Narrative Story
A Cosolvent-Powered Marketplace

Characters: Tomasz Wierzbicki - owner, precision screw machine shop, Windsor ON, Ananya Krishnamurthy - procurement engineer, automotive Tier-2 supplier, Kitchener ON

Act A - The Specification That Eliminates Most of the Market

Ananya Krishnamurthy is a procurement engineer at a Kitchener automotive Tier-2 supplier. She needs a prototype machining source for a hydraulic manifold fitting: 316L stainless steel, gun-drilled intersecting cross-holes at 3:1 L/D ratio, bore tolerance ±0.015mm, 63 Ra surface finish on the sealing surfaces, 50-unit prototype run with FAIR documentation.

She has searched SupplyCore, MFG.com, and three regional directories. She's found 40 shops that claim to do stainless machining. Four claim gun-drilling capability. She needs to determine which of the four can achieve 3:1 L/D in 316L at ±0.015mm — a determination that requires reading their equipment list and having a technical conversation with their process engineer. That conversation, times four shops, will take three days she doesn't have.

Act B - The Story

Tomasz Wierzbicki runs a 12-person precision shop in Windsor. His grandfather built the business on screw machine work. Over thirty years, the shop has evolved into a specialty in gun-drilling and deep-hole boring operations — work that most shops won't touch and that Tomasz's dedicated Unisig gun-drilling cell handles with a waiting list in peak season. His 316L stainless experience includes hydraulic and fluidic fittings for aerospace and automotive under ISO 9001. His prototype capability is documented: AS9100-adjacent FAIR process, CMM inspection with full GD&T reporting.

Tomasz registered his capability profile on a NGen-sponsored MarketForge platform: gun-drilling to 5:1 L/D in stainless and titanium, ±0.012mm bore tolerance demonstrated, 316L certified stock program, prototype run availability.

Ananya enters her requirement on the same platform: 316L, gun-drilled cross-holes 3:1 L/D, ±0.015mm bore tolerance, 63 Ra, 50-unit FAIR. The platform extracts the critical matching parameters from her description.

The semantic match: Tomasz's documented 5:1 L/D capability exceeds Ananya's 3:1 requirement. Demonstrated bore tolerance ±0.012mm exceeds her ±0.015mm requirement. 316L certified stock program eliminates material sourcing risk. Current prototype slot availability confirmed.

The platform delivers a capability alignment brief to both parties. Ananya sees: this shop's documented gun-drilling capability exceeds your specification requirements in every critical parameter. Tomasz sees: a 50-unit prototype for a hydraulic manifold fitting in 316L that his shop has produced variants of many times.

Ananya sends a structured RFQ — generated by the platform from her original requirement — to Tomasz the same afternoon. Tomasz quotes the following day. The quote includes a first-article inspection plan, material certification package, and delivery schedule.

Ananya awards the prototype order three days after her original search.

Act C - Why This Market Stays Broken Without Infrastructure

Tomasz's capability is real, demonstrably superior to most alternatives, and priced competitively. It is also completely invisible to Ananya through any search mechanism that exists without the platform.

The mismatch is not a matter of marketing. Tomasz cannot write a website that communicates gun-drilling to 5:1 L/D in 316L at ±0.012mm bore tolerance in terms that a procurement engineer finds in a keyword search. And Ananya cannot specify her requirement in terms that a directory's category filters can resolve.

The match requires semantic interpretation of both sides — translating the engineer's part requirement into machining process parameters, and translating the shop's capability data into requirement-relevant terms.

Thin market infrastructure dissolves the vocabulary barrier that keeps specialty machining capability invisible to the buyers who need it.

Characters are fictional. The machining capability dynamics and platform architecture are real. DeeperPoint is building the infrastructure this story describes.
