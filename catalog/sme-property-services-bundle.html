The Thin Market Problem

Strata corporations and commercial property managers seek annual maintenance service agreements that cover all routine maintenance categories under a single contract with a single provider relationship, consolidated invoicing, and coordinated scheduling. No independent plumber, electrician, HVAC technician, landscaper, or commercial cleaner covers all of these categories. Large property service companies—FirstService Residential, Brookfield Residential, commercial maintenance chains—can offer this bundle because they have internal capacity in most trades or have pre-negotiated sub-trade relationships. Independent tradespeople with strong local reputations, competitive pricing, and faster response times than large companies cannot access these contracts because they cannot present the single-vendor bundle the strata council is looking for. Meanwhile, independent tradespeople in the same neighbourhood with complementary coverage areas are not connected to each other and have no mechanism to propose a joint service arrangement that competes with the large provider.

Dominant Forces
  • Offering complexity — a complete building services bundle requires certified coverage across at least plumbing, electrical (including ESA compliance in Ontario), HVAC/refrigeration, elevator (third-party contract), landscaping and snow removal, and commercial cleaning, each with the appropriate trade licensing and insurance
  • Insurance and liability — each trade requires trade-specific liability insurance, and a bundle proposal must demonstrate combined coverage adequate to the strata's property value
  • Geographic coverage alignment — all consortium partners must serve the same neighbourhood or building district for the bundle to be operationally coherent
  • Contract structure — property managers want a master agreement with one lead contractor accountable for all service coordination and emergency response, not a panel of independent vendors
  • Opacity — independent tradespeople with compatible service territories and complementary trade coverage are not organized in any format that allows them to discover each other for consortium formation
How DeeperPoint Helps

Semantic matching encodes trade firm profiles (trade certification and licensing, service territory by postal code range, insurance coverage tier, emergency response SLA, sub-trade role vs. lead contractor preference) against property manager opportunity signals (building type and age, service categories required, service territory, response time SLA, contract value, incumbent relationship). Geographic proximity filtering ensures consortium partners have overlapping service territories.

Economic Upside

Annual building services contracts for a mid-sized strata corporation represent $80,000–$250,000 in predictable maintenance revenue across trade categories. Independent tradespeople in those categories typically see highly variable, demand-driven revenue from individual service calls. A single strata building services contract provides one to two years of partially predictable base revenue—equivalent to the revenue certainty of having several long-term residential service agreements. Consortium access to strata and commercial property contracts represents a structural improvement in revenue stability for independent trades.

Narrative Story
The Building on Dunbar

Characters: Tom — licensed electrician and electrical contractor, Vancouver; excellent residential and commercial reputation, Linda — strata property manager, managing a 68-unit concrete high-rise on Dunbar Street, Vancouver

✎ This story is in draft.

Act A — The Annual Contract

Every strata council in Canada faces the same annual administrative burden: coordinating a roster of independent trades for routine maintenance, emergency response, and scheduled inspections across a building that requires plumbing, electrical, HVAC, elevator, landscaping, and cleaning service. The strata council members are volunteers. They do not want to manage five separate vendor relationships, five separate invoicing streams, and five separate emergency phone numbers at 11 PM on a Wednesday when the heating system fails.

Linda manages seventeen strata corporations. She prefers consolidated service agreements—one vendor responsible for coordinating all routine maintenance categories, with a single monthly invoice and a single point of escalation for emergencies. She issued an RFP to four property services companies for a 68-unit high-rise whose incumbent had become unresponsive.

Three large companies responded. All three could produce the consolidated agreement she needed. Only two had responsive track records in the Vancouver building services market. She went with the lesser of two evils.

Act B — The Story

Tom has been servicing electrical systems in residential and commercial buildings in the Dunbar neighbourhood for eleven years. His response time is reliably under three hours for emergency calls. His pricing is below the large company rates. Three strata councils where he does individual service calls have asked him informally if he does building service agreements. He has always said he only does electrical, which is true—he cannot offer the full scope the councils are looking for.

On the MarketForge property services consortium platform, Tom had assembled a local consortium three months before Linda's RFP: himself for electrical, a licensed plumber with an overlapping service territory, an HVAC company with a preventive maintenance program for commercial buildings, a landscaping and snow removal company with several Dunbar-area building contracts, and a commercial cleaning company with strata experience.

He had not actively bid a strata contract yet. He was waiting for the right opportunity to test the consortium proposal approach.

Linda's RFP was distributed through a strata management industry newsletter that Tom subscribed to. He saw it the morning it was posted.

He assembled the consortium proposal in two days using the platform's master agreement template. The proposal quoted a combined annual rate 18% below the large company's comparable package—because none of the five consortium partners carried the overhead of a national property services operation.

Linda read the proposal and saw a risk: she had never managed a multi-provider consortium at this level of formalization before. The master agreement structure addressed her concerns about single-point accountability—Tom's company was the lead contractor, responsible for coordinating all trade responses and producing consolidated monthly reporting.

She called two references Tom provided. Both were residential condo clients where he had arranged informal multi-trade coverage for emergency response. Both gave strong references.

She awarded the consortium the contract.

Act C — Why This Market Stays Broken Without Infrastructure

Tom's consortium existed before Linda's RFP. The member firms were vetted, insured, and serving overlapping territories. The only thing missing was a mechanism that made Tom's assembled consortium visible to Linda's procurement process when the RFP was issued.

Without the platform, Tom would not have seen the RFP in time. Without the master agreement template, the consortium proposal would have been an informal letter that Linda's board would not have accepted. Without the geographic proximity search that assembled compatible firms in the first place, Tom would not have known which plumber, HVAC company, or landscaper had complementary Dunbar coverage.

Thin market infrastructure provides the pre-formation infrastructure—the discovery, the assembly, and the proposal framework—that turns five independent trade businesses into a competitive building services consortium without requiring them to merge or give up their independence.

Characters are fictional. Strata property management conventions in British Columbia, ESA electrical contractor licensing, and strata building services contract structures are real. DeeperPoint is building the infrastructure this story describes.
