The Thin Market Problem

Refugee Protection Division hearings at the Immigration and Refugee Board require two categories of scarce expertise: country conditions experts who have current, specific knowledge of the political, security, and social conditions in an applicant's country of origin; and interpreters who are competent in not just the national language but the specific regional dialect, minority language, or cross-border linguistic variant the applicant uses. A country conditions expert on Nigeria is not interchangeable with an expert on Delta State targeting of Ijaw environmental activists. An interpreter qualified in Sudanese Arabic is not interchangeable with a Nuer interpreter for a South Sudanese Nuer applicant. These distinctions are legally consequential — country conditions errors and interpreter accuracy failures are among the most frequent grounds for successful Federal Court judicial review of refugee determinations. Yet the IRB's capacity to identify the right expert and the right interpreter for a specific hearing remains limited by a roster system that has not kept pace with the diversity of current refugee claimant origins.

Dominant Forces
  • Participant scarcity — qualified country conditions experts with current, specific sub-national knowledge of conflict zones and minority community targeting are a small and poorly organized population in Canada
  • Dialect specificity — the distinction between a qualified national language interpreter and a qualified minority dialect or regional variant interpreter is legally consequential and not captured in standard interpreter registries
  • Offering complexity — matching requires alignment on country of origin, specific region and ethnicity, claimed persecution basis (ethnic, political, religious, gender-based), hearing urgency tier, and interpreter dialect classification
  • Knowledge timeliness — country conditions change rapidly in active conflict zones; an expert whose knowledge is three years out of date may provide testimony that misrepresents current conditions
  • Access to justice — errors in expert and interpreter matching systematically disadvantage claimants from minority linguistic and ethnic communities with limited diaspora presence in Canada
How DeeperPoint Helps

Semantic matching encodes expert profiles (country expertise by region, ethnicity, and persecution basis type; most recent in-country or primary source research; court qualification history) and interpreter profiles (language family, dialect classification, regional variant, IRB certification status, security check levels) against hearing demand signals (country, region, ethnicity, persecution basis, hearing date, urgency tier, official vs. minority language requirement). KnowledgeSlot curates IRB Refugee Protection Division rules of procedure, UNHCR country of origin information reports, and the IRB's country conditions expert guideline.

Economic Upside

Federal Court judicial review of IRB decisions costs $15,000–$50,000 per proceeding and results in a significant proportion of successful applications being returned for re-determination — adding 18–36 months to resolution timelines for applicants who are frequently in precarious status. Better expert and interpreter matching at the hearing stage reduces the substantive errors that produce successful judicial reviews, improves the accuracy of determinations, and reduces the systemic costs of re-determination cycles.

Narrative Story
The Nuer Word

Characters: Abuk — Refugee Protection claimant, South Sudanese Nuer, Jonglei State origin, Dr. Amelia — country conditions expert, South Sudan, Nuer community targeting post-2016 conflict, University of Ottawa

Act A — The Dialect Problem

Language is not a monolith. Sudan and South Sudan together contain more than sixty distinct languages and numerous additional dialects. A Dinka-speaking interpreter and a Nuer-speaking claimant do not share a language. They share a country of origin on a map. That is not the same thing.

The IRB's interpreter registry has evolved significantly over the past two decades. It includes interpreters qualified in Somali, Tigrinya, Amharic, Pashto, Dari, Arabic — the national-level languages of major refugee origin countries. What it does not always have — because demand is episodic and the communities are small — are interpreters qualified in specific minority languages within those countries, or interpreters who can be matched to the specific regional dialect variant an individual claimant uses.

When an interpreter and a claimant meet at the hearing table and the interpreter speaks a language the claimant cannot fully follow, the hearing proceeds anyway. The hearing officer does not know the difference. The claimant may not understand that they have the right to object. The record reflects a completed, interpreted hearing.

The Federal Court reviewer who reads the transcript may identify, from the claimant's attorney's notes or a subsequent sworn statement, that communication was significantly impaired.

Re-determination means starting over. For the claimant, it means another year or two of uncertainty.

Act B — The Story

Abuk fled Jonglei State in South Sudan in 2022 following targeted violence against her community that she described in detail to her legal aid lawyer: specific incidents, specific perpetrator groups, specific village locations. Her lawyer filed a claim under the refugee protection provisions citing ethnic targeting of Nuer civilians by government-aligned militia in the post-2016 conflict period.

The IRB scheduled her hearing. The registry provided an interpreter described as "South Sudanese Arabic." Abuk's primary language is Nuer. Her South Sudanese Arabic is limited to market-level commerce phrases. The interpreter conducted the hearing in South Sudanese Arabic.

The hearing officer noted in the record that the claimant appeared hesitant and gave limited detail in response to specific questions about the targeting incidents. The determination was negative — credibility was assessed as insufficient on the specific incident descriptions.

Abuk's lawyer filed for Federal Court judicial review. The application included a sworn statement from Abuk's community pastor, a fluent Nuer speaker, attesting that Abuk had described the incidents in detail in Nuer but had been unable to convey them accurately through the South Sudanese Arabic interpretation.

The Federal Court set aside the determination and ordered re-hearing with a Nuer-qualified interpreter and a South Sudan country conditions expert with post-2016 Nuer community targeting expertise specifically.

The IRB coordinator searched the interpreter registry for Nuer. Three profiles: one certified, two listed as "proficient." The certified interpreter's most recent IRB engagement was three years prior.

The coordinator logged into the MarketForge interpreter and expert matching platform. Nuer-qualified, IRB-certified, available within six weeks: two results — one in Ottawa, one willing to conduct by video link from Toronto.

The expert search: South Sudan, Nuer community, post-2016 conflict, ethnic targeting, current knowledge. Dr. Amelia's profile came back first. Her most recent primary source research — interviews with Nuer displaced community members in Uganda — was eight months old. She had testified at two IRB hearings on South Sudan and been qualified as an expert by the Federal Court.

The re-hearing was scheduled.

Abuk's protection claim was granted.

Act C — Why This Market Stays Broken Without Infrastructure

The Nuer-qualified interpreter and the South Sudan country conditions expert with Nuer community expertise existed in Canada before the original hearing. The IRB registry did not surface them because the registry's search categories — country of origin, national language — do not capture the sub-national linguistic and ethnic distinctions that determine interpreter-claimant compatibility and expert testimony relevance.

Abuk's incorrect determination, her Federal Court application, her re-determination — all of this was the consequence of a discovery failure at the point of hearing preparation. The right practitioner profiles existed. The match was not made.

Thin market infrastructure makes the distinction between a national-language interpreter and a minority-language-qualified interpreter searchable before the hearing, not discoverable after the re-determination order.

Characters are fictional. The IRB Refugee Protection Division procedures, Nuer language classification within South Sudan, and Federal Court judicial review grounds for interpreter error are real. DeeperPoint is building the infrastructure this story describes.
