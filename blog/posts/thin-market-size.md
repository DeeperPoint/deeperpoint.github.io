## The Question Nobody Has Answered

Every new market platform launches with a TAM slide. "The global $X market is worth $Y billion." These numbers are typically wrong — not because the market isn't large, but because the slide measures the *existing* market, not the *missing* one.

Thin markets are different. The whole point of a thin market is that willing buyers and sellers exist but cannot find each other, cannot evaluate each other, or cannot structure a deal even when they do. The transactions that *should happen* but *don't* — those are the opportunity. And nobody measures them, because by definition they leave no trace in the data.

We tried anyway. Using two independent methods — one bottom-up, one top-down — we estimated the total value of commerce currently lost to thin market friction. The results bracket the problem from both sides.

| | **Narrow Estimate** | **Wide Estimate** |
|---|---|---|
| **Method** | Bottom-up from 20 curated verticals | Top-down from full economy by sector |
| **Canada** | **$9–21B** (0.3–0.7% of GDP) | **$76–141B** (2.7–5.0% of GDP) |
| **Global** | **$290–610B** | **$5.7–10.4 trillion** |

Before you dismiss the larger number, understand what it includes — and what it doesn't. These are not market sizes. They are *missing transactions*: commerce that would create value for both parties if the matching infrastructure existed.

## Method 1: Start With What We Know (The Narrow Estimate)

DeeperPoint has documented [twenty specific thin market verticals](../examples.html) — from cross-border specialty grain to rare disease patient-to-trial matching — each with identified market sizes, dominant friction forces, and concrete DeeperPoint tool fit. We also have six published story posts that illustrate individual transactions at ground level.

The method is straightforward: for each vertical, take the global market size (from public industry data), estimate the "thin fraction" — the share that is structurally thin and addressable by AI-driven matching — and sum.

### What the Narrow Estimate Misses

The 20 verticals were selected to demonstrate *diversity and DeeperPoint tool fit*, not to maximise value coverage. They deliberately exclude mainstream construction subcontractor matching, routine professional services, most of the labour market, government procurement, and dozens of other sectors where thin market conditions exist.

The adjacency multiplier (1.5–2×) is a fudge factor. It is not analytically derived. Which brings us to Method 2.

## Method 2: Scan the Whole Economy (The Wide Estimate)

What if we start from the full economy — every NAICS sector — and ask: *what fraction of potential transactions in each sector fail to occur because of thin market forces?*

### The Logic

```
Sector GDP
  × Transaction intensity (what share involves matching-dependent transactions?)
  × Thin market fraction (what share suffers from thin market forces?)
  × Addressability (what share is reducible by AI-driven matching?)
  = Recoverable thin market transaction value
```

### Anchoring to the Academic Literature

This isn't speculation. Wallis and North (1988) measured total transaction costs in the U.S. economy at approximately **55% of GDP** — all costs of using the price mechanism: search, negotiation, monitoring, enforcement. Similar studies found 60% in Australia (1991) and 35% in Argentina (1990).

Thin market costs are a *subset* of those transaction costs: specifically, the costs of transactions that **fail to occur at all**. Not "expensive transactions" but "missing transactions." Our estimate should be well below 55% of GDP — and it is.

### Canada, Sector by Sector

Applied to all 20 NAICS two-digit sectors plus cross-border trade and labour market mismatch as overlay categories, the sector-by-sector analysis yields:

| Cross-border trade | $800B | $16.2–29.7B |
| Manufacturing | $207B | $6.8–12.3B |
| Labour market mismatch | $1,400B (employment income) | $5.9–12.6B |
| Construction | $165B | $4.8–8.8B |

**Professional services** is the single largest domestic source — every SME that pays a generalist because they can't find the specialist they need. **Cross-border trade** is the largest overall — reflecting the geometric scaling of thin markets across bilateral corridors.

### Global, by Economy Group

For the global estimate, two adjustments scale the Canadian model:

**Developing economies have thicker thin markets.** Weaker information infrastructure, more severe regulatory fragmentation, less developed trust institutions. The Ethiopia produce story quantifies this: 30% post-harvest loss, 50%+ information asymmetry margin capture.

**Cross-border thin markets scale geometrically.** With ~195 countries, the potential bilateral trade corridors vastly exceed any single country's. Most carry zero trade *specifically because* of thin market conditions.

| Economy Group | GDP | Thin Market % | Recoverable Value |
|---|---|---|---|

| Low-income & fragile states | $5T | 6.0–10.0% | $0.3–0.5T |
| Cross-border (incremental) | $30T trade vol. | 8–15% | $2.4–4.5T |
| **Total** | | | **$5.7–10.4T** |

## Why the Two Numbers Are Both Right

| | Narrow | Wide | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Canada** | $9–21B | $76–141B | **~8×** |
| **Global** | $290–610B | $5.7–10.4T | **~17×** |

The 8–17× gap is not a contradiction. It is a measure of *coverage*.

The narrow estimate says: "Here are 20 verticals we can describe in detail, with concrete evidence. They are worth this much." The wide estimate says: "Here is what the entire thin market problem is worth, including the thousands of micro-markets the 20 examples don't touch."

The internal consistency check works: Canada's wide estimate (2.7–5.0% of GDP) represents 5–9% of Wallis and North's total transaction cost estimate — plausible for the fraction where AI-driven matching could create transactions that don't currently exist. The global estimate is proportionally higher because developing economies have structurally thicker thin markets and cross-border friction scales geometrically.

---

## What "Recoverable" Means — and Doesn't

These figures represent **incremental transaction value**: commerce that would create value for both parties but currently does not occur. This is distinct from:

- **Total market size** — most of each market works through existing channels
- **Revenue to a platform operator** — platform fees would be a small fraction of transaction value
- **GDP contribution** — which would include multiplier effects, typically 2–3× direct transaction value

The GDP contribution from unlocking this value — accounting for employment, tax revenue, input purchases, and reduced waste — would be substantially larger than the direct transaction figures.

---

## The Actionable Takeaway

**For sponsors and investors**, the narrow estimate is the more actionable number. It identifies 20 specific verticals with $290–610B in recoverable global transaction value, grounded in documented market sizes and concrete per-transaction evidence.

**For policymakers**, the wide estimate provides the strategic frame. Thin markets are not a niche phenomenon or a curiosity of exotic commodities. They are a structural feature of every economy — representing 2.7–5.0% of Canadian GDP and 5.2–9.5% of global GDP in missing commerce. They intensify in developing countries and across borders, which is precisely where the economic and social returns to solving them are highest.

**For the discipline of market engineering**, the ratio between the two estimates — 8–17× — is itself the finding. It says that even a comprehensive catalogue of 20 carefully selected examples captures only 6–12% of the total thin market landscape. The long tail is enormous, and every sector has one.
