Market Scenario: Safely Searching the Dark

The Innovation Paradox

For deep tech entrepreneurs and R&D engineers, intellectual property is not just an asset—it is the entire foundation of their company. Whether they are developing novel metamaterials, advanced genomic therapies, or solid-state batteries, the core value of their work lies in the secrets they hold.

This creates a brutal paradox. To bring a deep tech innovation to market, founders must collaborate. They need to find complementary technologies, specialized manufacturing partners, and co-developers. But the moment they start describing what they are looking for, they risk leaking the very IP they are trying to protect. If you go to a public forum or even a closed industry group and ask, “Has anyone solved the thermal runaway problem for zirconium-doped electrolytes?”, you have just told your competitors exactly what you are working on.

Mateo Primorac, founder and CEO of scopri.ai, understands this problem intimately. His company already automates highly complex technological knowledge discovery, helping engineers analyze patents in minutes. But what if that capability could be turned inward to a private community of European technology entrepreneurs, like those supported by the European Institute of Innovation & Technology (EIT)?

How do you match founders who have complementary IP concerns or ideas, without ever forcing them to ingest their sensitive data into a centralized, searchable database?

This is exactly the type of trust deficit and opacity that the DeeperPoint Cosolvent architecture was designed to solve. By separating the orchestration of a match from the raw data itself, Cosolvent allows participants to safely search the dark. Let’s look at how such a tool might work in practice.

The Story: The Munich-Stockholm Handshake

Elara sat in her lab at the Technical University of Munich, staring at the latest readouts from her impedance spectrometer. She had done it. She had synthesized a novel polymer-ceramic composite electrolyte that maintained high ionic conductivity at room temperature. It was a breakthrough for solid-state batteries.

But there was a problem: scaling it. Elara was a brilliant chemist, but she knew nothing about roll-to-roll manufacturing. Her lab-scale synthesis was practically artisanal. If she couldn’t figure out how to produce the composite in continuous sheets, her breakthrough would remain an academic curiosity.

She needed a process engineer who had specifically worked with high-viscosity ceramic slurries. But she couldn’t just post a request on an EIT job board. The specific combination of keywords—“continuous coating,” “high-viscosity composite,” “room temperature conductivity”—would immediately signal to her well-funded competitors exactly how close she was to a commercial battery.

Meanwhile, twelve hundred kilometers away in Stockholm, Jens was frustrated. He had spent the last three years developing a proprietary slot-die coating process capable of depositing highly abrasive, viscous slurries with micron-level precision. It was an incredible piece of manufacturing IP. But to prove commercial viability and secure his next round of funding, he needed to test it on a next-generation battery material. He needed a partner with a novel electrolyte, but cold-calling battery startups and asking for their most guarded chemical formulations was a non-starter.

Both Elara and Jens were members of an EIT deep tech community. And both were using a new, Cosolvent-derived IP matchmaking tool developed in partnership with scopri.ai.

Defining the Edges

Elara logged into the platform. Unlike traditional networks that ask users to fill out publicly searchable profiles, this tool—sponsored by EIT—provided a strictly governed, secure repository.

Elara securely submitted the detailed “edges” of her problem into the repository. She described her specific chemical formula and manufacturing constraints. However, the EIT platform operated under strict semantic matching rules enforced by the Cosolvent engine. The scopri.ai layer, acting as a highly specialized CommonContext, was instructed to ingest this data only for the purpose of semantic matching.

The system was architecturally constrained. The AI could “read” Elara’s needs to understand the structural shape of the problem she was trying to solve, but it was hard-coded to never reveal that raw data to any counterparty or other system users without her express, stage-gated approval.

In Stockholm, Jens had done the same. He had uploaded the detailed capabilities of his slot-die coating process and the constraints of the test materials he needed into the secure EIT repository. His IP was locked in the vault, accessible only to the blind semantic matching engine.

The Blind Match

Deep within the secure environment, the Cosolvent matching engine was constantly reviewing these confidential profiles. It wasn’t exposing keyword searches to users; it was using AI to look for complementary structural shapes in the technological landscape.

On a Tuesday afternoon, the engine found an alignment. The logic of Elara’s scaling problem perfectly matched the capabilities of Jens’s manufacturing process.

The platform did not reveal Elara’s identity to Jens, nor Jens’s identity to Elara. Instead, it initiated a blind handshake. Both received a notification: A highly probable complementary match has been found regarding [High-Viscosity Slurry Deposition / Novel Electrolyte Synthesis]. Probability of match relevance: 94%.

Structured Disclosure

This was the critical moment. In a traditional market, one party would have to take a leap of faith and share sensitive information first. The Cosolvent architecture replaced this leap of faith with a structured, step-by-step disclosure protocol.

Both Elara and Jens clicked “Proceed.” The platform generated an automated, mutual Non-Disclosure Agreement, customized based on the overlapping parameters of their confidential profiles. Neither had to pay legal fees to draft it; the CommonContext layer ensured it was compliant with both EU and national IP frameworks.

Before the NDA was signed — while both parties were still fully pseudonymous — the platform generated a Generative Match Story. This scenario was careful: it described the structural shape of the collaboration, not its content. No polymer formulas. No process parameters. Only the logical framework of how a partnership between a materials breakthrough and a manufacturing process capability typically works at this stage of deep tech development.

The scenario described a joint testing agreement in which each party retains sole ownership of their respective IP; any resulting patents on the combination are jointly filed; a three-month validation program tests whether the process can handle the material at scale; results are shared exclusively between the two parties under the NDA. The scenario also named the specific legal instrument — a bilateral R&D collaboration agreement under German-Swedish cross-border IP law — and identified the EIT community’s standard boilerplate for exactly this structure.

Neither Elara nor Jens had to ask “what does a collaboration like this even look like?” The platform had already answered that question, in structural terms, before a single proprietary detail was disclosed. When they clicked “Proceed” and the NDA was generated, they were signing a document whose framework they had already reviewed — not committing to an unknown process.

Once the NDA was digitally signed by both pseudonymous parties, they entered a secure Cosolvent deal-room. Only now were their identities, institutional affiliations, and platform-generated, non-confidential summaries revealed to one another—just enough information to verify the platform’s match.

Elara read Jens’s summary. A novel slot-die process achieving 2-micron tolerance on abrasive ceramic slurries. Her heart skipped a beat. It was exactly what she needed.

Jens read Elara’s summary. A room-temperature polymer-ceramic composite ready for roll-to-roll testing. It was the perfect validation material.

Through the deal-room, they negotiated the terms of a joint testing agreement. Elara would retain sole ownership of her chemical IP; Jens would retain sole ownership of his process IP. Any resulting patents on the combination would be jointly filed. The platform provided the boilerplate legal architecture for this complex IP interaction, dramatically lowering the transaction costs that would normally kill such a deal before it started.

Only when both were fully satisfied with the legal and operational framework did they authorize the deep sharing of their respective data. Elara finally sent the viscosity parameters of her electrolyte. Jens sent his flow-rate specifications.

They had found the perfect partner, completely in the dark, without ever exposing their secrets to the open market.

Structural Summary

The scenario between Elara and Jens illustrates a classic thin market constrained by two powerful forces: Trust Deficit and Information Asymmetry.

In the deep tech sector, IP is a fragile asset. The need for absolute confidentiality creates extreme opacity. Participants cannot openly signal their needs or capabilities, making it impossible for traditional market mechanisms (like search engines or open job boards) to match supply and demand. The cost of finding a partner without leaking IP is so high that many potential collaborations simply never happen, stranding valuable innovations in the lab.

A Cosolvent-derived matching architecture elegantly solves this dilemma. By decoupling the matching process from public disclosure, the platform acts as a blind broker. The sponsor—in this case, EIT—establishes a secure repository for contributors’ information. The semantic matching AI is instructed to use that information strictly for finding complementary alignments. It serves as an infallible intermediary that will never reveal a user’s IP to any counterparty or other system users without the owner’s express, affirmative consent.

When a match is found, the platform lowers the barrier to collaboration by staging the disclosure process. It provides the legal scaffolding (NDAs, joint development agreements) precisely when it is needed, bridging the trust deficit step-by-step. For a community builder like Mateo Primorac working with European tech entrepreneurs, this architecture offers a way to generate massive network effects while strictly protecting the crown jewels of every member.
