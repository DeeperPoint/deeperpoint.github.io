A second opinion

Should I Take This Seriously?

Every project describes itself well. We asked an AI model to assess this one, under a written instruction to be balanced and honest about its strengths and its weaknesses. This page gives the answer as the model wrote it, the instruction it was given, and the same question for you to put to any model you choose.

How this was written

Claude Fable 5.2, an AI model made by Anthropic, wrote this assessment on 24 November 2026 at the request of DeeperPoint’s founder. It is commissioned, not independent. The model was told not to favour the project or its founder, and the instruction is printed in full below.

A second, separate run of the same model then checked each sentence against the sources, and its corrections were applied. They went both ways: some softened a judgment that was harsher than the sources, and some removed a claim in the project’s favour that the sources did not support. Two of the four sources are the project’s own files and are not public, and the assessment says where it leans on them.

The founder has not edited the text. He has changed the instruction twice, each time after reading a version, and each time the assessment was written afresh and checked again. The first change asked that the comparison keep to thin markets, leaving out products made for markets that are already busy, and that the model judge the claim that one answer can serve many markets. The second asked the model to judge the project’s choice to offer a catalog of worked ideas in place of working markets, which it cannot yet show, and to place the evidence on a ladder. The first version also pointed out places where this site contradicted itself, and those pages were corrected.

  • Is the thinking behind this reasonable and credible?
  • Does anyone else offer a comparable or better approach, and at what cost?
  • Where does this approach shine, and where should I look for another approach?
  • Do not take our model’s word for it: ask one yourself

The short answer

DeeperPoint’s account of why some markets stay thin, meaning willing buyers and sellers exist but rarely find, trust and deal with each other, rests on established economics, and its proposal, one reusable set of software and methods for many such markets, is a reasonable hypothesis that nobody outside the project has tested. No real market runs on it: there are no real participants, no completed deals, and no organization has yet agreed to run one. What exists is early software, built and under test with invented participants, a catalog of 274 market ideas of which 20 have been researched against sources, and one founder who pays for the work himself. A visitor who needs deals soon should use a broker or an existing service; a visitor who knows one market deeply and can accept going first has something real to try.

Is the thinking behind this reasonable and credible?

Three sources used here are not public: the project’s profile, its originality file and its records of catalog work. Statements resting on them are marked.

Where the ideas come from. The site traces the problem to eighty years of economics: Coase on transaction costs, Akerlof on quality a buyer cannot observe, Roth on markets that need deliberate design. That research is established. The originality file (not public) says thin-market theory “is established economics” and “DeeperPoint did not invent it”.

The project’s own contributions are three. One is a table, the intervention matrix, that sorts the obstacles into fourteen challenges and claims seven AI techniques answer them. The second is a design: an “engine”, meaning shared matching software that stays the same from market to market, fed by a “wiki”, a set of sourced pages holding what one market’s insiders know. The third is two documents the engine writes: a Content Match Story, telling both sides why they might deal before either is named, and a Deal Brief, a non-binding summary for the parties’ own lawyers and bankers. The originality file finds “No documented precedent” for the matrix and for those documents, and rates the matching itself “Not original”. Those verdicts are the project’s, drawn up in May 2026 and reviewed against web sources on 5 November 2026; competitor facts were re-checked on 21 November 2026, and the academic literature was not. I made no search of my own.

Has anyone outside tested the central claim? No. The sources record no published paper, no peer review (checking by independent specialists) and no outside trial. The critiques the originality file lists are, going by their titles, the work of AI models. The founder, Mustafa (Vic) Uzumeri, holds a PhD and is emeritus after a 25-year academic career (profile, not public). The About page records a textbook on product families, journal articles on a quality standard and on worker learning, practical work on trucking and grain markets, and nothing published on market design.

The generality claim. The site says the frictions “are common across thousands of markets”, so “the response can be built once and reused”, with a builder adding only the market’s own business logic. The first half is sound: the cited research treats search costs, mistrust and hard-to-describe offerings as features of markets in general, not of one sector. The second half is the project’s inference, and I find the evidence for it limited in three ways.

  • The site says the seven techniques “collectively address every friction in the table above”. It also says “Three are built today: matching, the trusted intermediary and input translation.” The profile says memory across sessions, pooling of small suppliers and protection against currency and tariff swings are not built.
  • Reuse has been tried on two invented markets, a machining capacity exchange and a job-matching market. In both, the list of facts recorded about each participant was written by hand, and the originality file says it did not check whether the engine’s code had to change. The cost page says a second build was expected to cost about two-thirds of the first and its plan came out higher: “The saving from experience has not been seen yet.”
  • On 24 November 2026 the project labelled 254 catalog ideas with the challenges that hold each back (record not public; fifteen labels, the fourteen plus one facet of hidden information counted separately). Hidden information was labelled on 225 and hard-to-describe offerings on 171; these, with mistrust on 99, are the problems the three built techniques address. Five of the fifteen labels appeared on two ideas or none. On 85 ideas the pass also noted that one side is scarce, a force the framework sets aside; where scarcity is the real constraint, matching cannot help, and the record does not say in how many it is.

I conclude the premise is plausible for a core of problems (finding the other party, describing an offer, disclosing safely), which are also the built parts, though tried only on two invented markets, and is untested beyond it.

The evidence choice. The catalog’s index says: “Any single idea can be argued with. The point is how many plausible ones there are, across unrelated sectors, places and kinds of participant.” A catalog of this kind is evidence that the framework can describe many situations in one vocabulary. It is not evidence that any of those markets would form, that people would join, or that the obstacles named are the decisive ones. The ideas were written by applying the framework, so the fact that it fits them shows it can be applied widely, not that it is right. The circle is not complete: the labelling pass recorded forces outside the framework, and research overturned some proposed labels. The project’s own file (not public) concedes the limit: the ideas “establish generative range but do not constitute empirical validation.”

The research record (not public) shows how 20 ideas were produced. They began as short proposals written to illustrate five challenges the catalog barely covered. On 24 November 2026, five AI research runs wrote them up under a rule that every number have a source. Each run reported its own sources and no second run checked them. Seventeen of the 20 carry at least one fact taken from the snippet a search engine shows, because the source page would not open. Of the other 254 the sources say only that they were written earlier and “have not been researched since”; they do not say how, or who did the labelling.

I read the twelve sampled ideas. All twelve label their characters as fictional in a closing note; six also say so before the story begins. Eleven of the twelve analyses are specific, naming each market’s own bodies and rules; one is short and generic. The eleven older ideas give market sizes, counts and fees with no source (the index calls the market figures “estimates”). Most describe the software doing things the profile says are not built, such as verifying certificates and pooling suppliers. One asserts “exactly eleven” certified facilities without a source, and one story’s summary contradicts its ending. The single new idea is of a different standard: it names its sources, marks what is not built, and says “No source gives the value of engagements delayed or lost in onboarding, and none is estimated here.” The research record still calls its premise the “weakest premise of the twenty”.

Was the catalog a reasonable way to proceed? As a way to show range and let a visitor recognise their own market, yes, and it is more than assertion: each idea is specific enough to be argued with. As proof, other options were open. Testing the framework against past marketplaces that succeeded and failed, or asking people who work in a few of these markets to mark up the ideas, were also desk work, and neither was done. Research contradicted or failed to support 17 of the 20 new proposals, in seven cases only a secondary label. Those were sketches, not finished entries, so the rate does not carry over directly; but the older ideas carry unsourced figures of the same kind, and I would expect research to change many of them.

The ladder.

  1. Assertion: reached.
  2. Structured hypothetical cases: reached, with 274 ideas.
  3. Hypothetical cases with sourced facts: reached for 20 of 274, with the gaps above.
  4. Tested against real past cases: skipped. A project tool called MarketMap reads real companies through the framework, but the sources show no check of those readings against what happened.
  5. Review by people who know the markets: skipped. The site asks for such review and records none. The job-matching example drew on “casual conversations with people who know the field”; of one demonstration the site says “no support worker has reviewed one yet”.
  6. Working demonstration with invented participants: partly reached. Of its three “twins” (the engine filled with invented participants) the site says “All three are still being built.” One is a scripted walkthrough that computes nothing and is public; one has matches computed in advance and is not yet open; one computes matches live and is shown by request. The one measured result (profile, not public): in the job-matching twin, one wiki page opens a route for 10 of the 55 invented workers a certificate requirement would block.
  7. Pilot, meaning a trial with real participants: not reached. There is no sponsor, meaning an organization that would run a market.
  8. Markets in operation: not reached.

Rungs 4 and 5 need no adopter. Finishing rung 3 means researching the other 254 ideas and re-opening the unverified facts. Rung 6 means finishing a twin that outsiders can operate. Rung 7 needs a sponsor.

Does anyone else offer a comparable or better approach, and at what cost?

Only answers to a thin market are compared here; tools for markets that are already busy are left out. The originality file reports finding no other product that combines a free shared engine, a market’s own knowledge base and confidential matching in a form another organization could run. That search was the project’s own, on the dates given above. Brokers, associations and Boardy work today with real participants, which DeeperPoint does not. Left out: Sharetribe, a general marketplace builder, because it assumes a market busy enough for listings and keyword search (Build plan US$39 a month, Live plan US$259 a month or US$199 when billed yearly, plus usage, checked 21 November 2026); Xometry, which quotes standard parts in a busy market, and its Thomas Smart Search, which serves buyers searching an existing base of suppliers; and Pactum, which negotiates for one large buyer. GrowinCo, which matches brands with manufacturers that have unused production lines, is the nearest platform for a single thin market in the project’s file; the file does not say whether it runs with real participants, so I have not ranked it.

What adopting DeeperPoint costs. Free to license is not free to adopt. The code is open source, meaning published for anyone to use, change and copy, and there is no fee; one of its four bodies of code, the generator of invented participants, carries a condition: invented and real participants are never mixed. The site (24 November 2026) estimates that a new wiki and a first twin take 215 to 355 hours of skilled work plus about US$100 to US$1,200 in AI fees and tools. The page does not split the hours between the developer and the market expert, and they buy a demonstration, not a running market. The site says the figures leave out recruiting participants, legal review, support, operations and keeping the wiki current. On their reliability it says: “Not very, yet, and it is better to say so.” It adds: “Nobody logged hours on any of them.”

The costs that are not money: the expert’s judgment cannot be delegated; for a real marketplace the adopter must supply its own screens, payments and contracts (the demonstration’s screens are provided); and the adopter goes first. The project’s four active code repositories (public folders of code) together show one star, a bookmark from a user, and no forks, meaning copies taken to develop separately; one of the four has been public for six days.

Where does this approach shine, and where should I look for another approach?

What works today. Per the profile (not public; engine checked 28 October 2026), built and under test, with invented participants: matching from plain-language descriptions, in which one side can search for the other; keeping details hidden until both sides agree to reveal them; reading documents into profiles; the Content Match Story and Deal Brief; wiki tools; a question page over one wiki. Demonstrations: three twins, all unfinished, as described above. Not built: memory, pooling, risk protection, matching that weighs both sides’ preferences together, verification of credentials, and any marketplace with real participants.

It is the stronger choice when:

  • you know one thin market well, can commit an expert’s time and a developer, and want to watch the market behave with invented participants before asking anything of real ones;
  • offers cannot be reduced to a listing and the parties will not reveal details up front;
  • you want to own the code and the knowledge, with the right to carry on without the project;
  • you want only the diagnosis, which is free to read.

Choose something else when:

  • you need deals this year: a broker, an agent, or your association’s introductions;
  • the goods are standard and buyers already search for them: your market is probably not thin, and a hosted marketplace builder such as Sharetribe is made for that;
  • you want introductions among founders and investors: Boardy;
  • you are sourcing industrial suppliers in North America: Thomas Smart Search;
  • the real problem is too few participants: no matching tool supplies them;
  • nobody who knows the market can commit the time: do not start.

Who it depends on. The site says the founder is “retired, self-funded, and answerable to no board or investors”. The public record of the main engine shows four accounts changed its code, 116, 84, 58 and 30 times; the founder’s account is the one with 30. The record does not say who the others are or whether they remain involved. The work also relies on paid AI services from other companies. If the founder stopped, the code would stay public and usable, and nobody would be obliged to maintain it.

What this assessment cannot tell you

I am an AI model reading documents, commissioned by the founder. I am not a peer reviewer and not independent. I have not run the software: “built and under test” is the project’s own statement, in a document you cannot see.

I cannot know whether a market built this way would work. Nobody can until one is tried with real participants.

I checked no fact in any catalog idea against an outside source, and I read twelve of 274, only one of them from the researched twenty.

Facts about other services come from the project’s file as of 21 November 2026. I did not re-check them, and prices change.

Do not take our model’s word for it

We chose the model, wrote the instruction and gave it two files you cannot see. The check on that is to ask a model of your own choosing, which can read only what is public. Copy the question below into any AI assistant that can browse the web. 

I am looking at a project called DeeperPoint for the first time and deciding whether it deserves more of my time. Please read its website, https://deeperpoint.com (the home page and the pages on why markets do not form, what it is building, the intervention matrix, cost, demos, MarketForge and About), and its public code at https://github.com/DeeperPoint. Search for alternatives yourself; do not rely on the project's own account of its competitors.

The subject is thin markets: markets where willing buyers and sellers exist but rarely manage to find, trust and deal with each other. Judge the project, and every alternative, as an answer to that problem. Leave out products made for markets that are already busy, and general online marketplace builders, unless they demonstrate a practical way to stand up a working platform for a typical thin market; if you leave out one I would expect to see, say why in a line.

The project holds, citing economics research, that many of the challenges that keep markets thin are common to very different situations, so that an answer can be built once and reused across many markets. Judge whether that is sound and how far the evidence goes. For each alternative, say whether it could serve many different thin markets or was built for one.

The strongest proof of that claim would be many working markets built this way. None exists, and a new approach cannot show any before somebody adopts it. The project wrote a catalog of worked ideas for thin markets instead (https://deeperpoint.com/catalog/index.html). Judge that choice: read a dozen ideas picked at random, say what a catalog of this kind is evidence of and what it is not, and whether it was a reasonable way to proceed. Then place the project's evidence on this ladder, saying which rungs it has reached and which it has skipped: (1) assertion; (2) structured hypothetical cases; (3) hypothetical cases with sourced facts; (4) the framework tested against real past cases; (5) review by people who know the markets; (6) a working demonstration with invented participants; (7) a pilot with real participants; (8) markets in operation.

Then answer three questions:
1. Is the thinking behind this reasonable and credible?
2. Does anyone else offer a comparable or better approach, and at what cost?
3. Where does this approach shine, and where should I look for another approach?

Be balanced, thorough and honest about the project's strengths and weaknesses. Do not favour the project or its founder, and do not be harsher than the facts warrant either.
- State strengths and limitations equally flatly. If the honest answer to a question is lopsided, say so.
- Say whose judgment each evaluation is: the project claims, the site says, you conclude.
- Do not report plans as progress. Something that is not built is "not built".
- Name the alternatives I would really weigh, including the ones that are not software (a broker, an association's directory, doing nothing). For each, say what it does better than DeeperPoint today and what it costs, with the date of the price.
- Tell me plainly: whether any real market runs on it with real participants and completed deals; which parts work, which are demonstrations with invented participants, and which are not built; what adopting it would cost me beyond the licence; whether anyone outside the project has tested its central idea; and who the project depends on.
- Say what you could not check, and what you cannot judge from reading documents.
Use plain language. Begin with a short answer of three or four sentences.

A model working only from public pages will know less than ours did about what is built, and it may get facts wrong in either direction. Where its answer and this page disagree, the cost page, the demos page and the public code are the places to check. If it finds something this page should say and does not, tell us.

The instruction the model was given

In full, as given. Only the layout has been changed for this page.

Read the instruction

You are asked to assess the DeeperPoint initiative for a first-time visitor to its website, deeperpoint.com. The founder of DeeperPoint commissioned this assessment and will publish it on that website with this instruction printed beside it.

The visitor has just met the project and is deciding whether it deserves more of their time. They may be a founder, an investor, an industry association, a public official, a researcher or a developer. Assume they are not technical. They are asking three questions:

  1. Is the thinking behind this reasonable and credible?
  2. Does anyone else offer a comparable or better approach, and at what cost?
  3. Where does this approach shine, and where should I look for another approach?

The subject is thin markets. A thin market is one where willing buyers and sellers exist but rarely manage to find, trust and deal with each other. Judge DeeperPoint, and every alternative, as an answer to that problem. There are a great many products and designs for markets that are already busy, and for general online marketplaces. Leave them out unless they demonstrate a practical way to stand up a working platform for a typical thin market; if you leave out one a visitor would expect to see, say why in a line.

The generality claim. The project holds, citing the economics research, that many of the challenges that keep markets thin are common to very different situations, so that an answer to them can be built once and reused across many markets. Judge whether that premise is sound and how far the evidence for it goes. Then say, for each alternative, whether it exploits or can deal with that generality: could it serve many different thin markets, or was it built for one?

The evidence choice. The strongest proof of the generality claim would be many working markets built this way. None exists, and a new approach cannot show any before somebody adopts it. The project chose to write a catalog of worked ideas for thin markets instead, each with an analysis of what keeps that market thin and a short fictional story. Judge that choice. Say what a catalog of this kind is evidence of and what it is not evidence of, how these ideas were produced and checked so far as the sources show, and whether it was a reasonable way to proceed given what else the project could have done. Read the sample of ideas you are given and judge their quality yourself. Then place the project’s evidence as a whole on this ladder, say which rungs it has reached, which it has skipped, and what reaching the next one would take:

  1. assertion;
  2. structured hypothetical cases;
  3. hypothetical cases with sourced facts;
  4. the framework tested against real past cases, successes and failures;
  5. review by people who know the markets concerned;
  6. a working demonstration with invented participants;
  7. a pilot with real participants;
  8. markets in operation.

Be balanced, thorough and honest about DeeperPoint’s strengths and weaknesses. Do not favour the project or its founder. The assessment has failed if the visitor comes away more impressed, or less impressed, than the facts warrant, even if every sentence in it is true.

How to judge

  • State strengths and limitations equally flatly. Do not follow a limitation with a sentence that takes it back, or a strength with a reflexive doubt.
  • Do not manufacture balance. If the honest answer to a question is lopsided, say so.
  • Say whose judgment each evaluation is: the project claims, the site says, you conclude. Where you say nothing comparable was found, say how hard anyone looked.
  • Do not report plans as progress. Something that is not built is “not built”.
  • Say what you cannot judge. You are an AI model reading documents. You are not a peer reviewer, you have not run the software, and you cannot know whether a market built this way would work.
  • Words that sell are out (unique, revolutionary, breakthrough, powerful, cutting-edge, robust, seamless), and so are words that prejudge the other way.

What the answer must contain

  • For question 1: where the ideas come from, which of them are established and which are the project’s own, and whether anyone outside the project has tested the central claim.
  • For question 1, also: your judgment of the generality claim, and your judgment of the evidence choice, with the ladder.
  • For question 2: the alternatives for a thin market that a visitor would really weigh, by name, including the ones that are not software (a broker or agent, an association’s directory, doing nothing). For each: what it does better than DeeperPoint today, whether it can serve many different thin markets or one, and what it costs where a price is public, with the date of the price. Then what adopting DeeperPoint costs, including the costs that are not money.
  • For question 3: the situations where this approach is the stronger choice, and, as specifically, the situations where the visitor should choose something else and what that something is.
  • Stated plainly somewhere, whatever the answers are: whether any real market runs on it with real participants and completed deals; which parts work, which are demonstrations with invented participants, and which are not built; that free to license is not the same as free to adopt; whether the central idea has been tested by anyone outside the project; and who the project depends on and what that means for its continuity.

Form

  • Plain language a non-technical reader could repeat to a colleague. Explain a project term once, in a clause, the first time it appears. No model names, databases or version numbers.
  • Begin with “The short answer”: three or four sentences that would survive being the only thing read, and that include the most important limitation.
  • Then one section for each of the three questions, with the question as its heading.
  • End with “What this assessment cannot tell you”.
  • 1,700 to 2,300 words. A table only if a comparison needs one.
  • Every date, price and count comes from the sources. Give each price with its date.

Sources (the commissioned assessment)

Use these and nothing from memory:

  • the public website as it stood on the day, including its home, platform, market-physics, intervention-matrix, cost, demos, MarketForge and About pages;
  • the project’s own profile document, which records what is built and what is not, with dates (not public);
  • the project’s own file on originality and competitors, last checked against public sources on 21 November 2026 (not public);
  • the catalog itself: its index page, and a sample of twelve of its ideas drawn at random by a fixed seed, not chosen by anyone (the list is published with this instruction);
  • the project’s records of work done on the catalog on 24 November 2026: the totals from a pass that tagged every idea with the challenges that bind it, and the research record for twenty ideas added that day (not public);
  • the public record of the project’s code repositories at github.com/DeeperPoint: which are public, their licences, how many people have written the code, and how much outside attention they show.

Where a statement rests only on a source the visitor cannot see, say so.

The checked text is about 2,700 words, over the 2,300 the instruction allowed, because the corrections added detail and none was cut to make room. The clause about how many people have written the code was added to the list of sources after the first check found it mattered. The twelve catalog ideas the model read were drawn by a fixed random seed (20261124) over the catalog’s file names: allergen-free co-manufacturing, art restoration specialists, forces-to-civilian employment, captive insurance formation, police oversight investigators, nearshore software, sustainable packaging, youth sport recruitment, municipal surplus assets, veterinary specialist referral, supplier onboarding at large corporations and remote renewable microgrids.
