Markets don't make themselves.

Thousands of markets never form: willing buyers and sellers who cannot find each other, trust each other, or close a deal. We call them thin markets. Eighty years of economics explains why they stay that way, and the frictions that keep them thin can now be engineered away.

1 · The problem · Why Markets Don’t Form

Why don’t some markets form?

Buyers and sellers who cannot find, trust or deal with each other. A capable shop with an idle machine and a buyer who needs exactly that machine can sit an hour apart and never meet.

The frictions are known, documented over eighty years, and they repeat across thousands of markets.

1 · The problem · Who Should Care

Is this my problem?

A thin market looks different from each seat. If one of these sounds like you, it probably is.

  • “My marketplace has too few people on either side to feel real.” Founders
  • “Several of my portfolio companies stall the same way.” Investors
  • “My members would trade with each other if they could do it without showing their hand.” Associations and sponsors
  • “We signed the agreement. The trade didn’t follow.” Trade officials
  • “The barrier came down and I still can’t find a buyer one province over.” Canadian businesses
  • “Our best matches depend on what a few experienced staff carry in their heads.” Workforce and service agencies
  • “My cohort keeps hitting the same wall.” Accelerators
  • “The companies that could use our work don’t know we exist.” Researchers
  • “Users find each other on our platform, then close the deal somewhere else.” Platform developers

1 · The problem · Your market

What about my market?

Start with a sentence about a market that should exist: who has something, and who needs it. You will see the closest ideas in the catalog, and what tends to stand in the way of markets like it.

Matched in your browser against the catalog. No AI model runs, and nothing you type is sent or stored unless you choose to send it.

Already have a venture? Ask for a MarketMap.

A MarketMap reads a company’s public web page, and any documents you add, and sets the venture against the same frictions: which challenges bind it, which interventions could release them, and how strong the evidence is. The frictions it names are the ones a market’s wiki, then its twin, then the marketplace itself would be built to address.

An AI-written analysis of public material, so check it against what you know. Running one is by request: ask for a code on the demos page and it is emailed to you at once. A code covers one map.

2 · The solution · What We’re Building

What is DeeperPoint building?

A free, open-source market engine, built once and dressed for each market: semantic matching, confidential disclosure, and a Deal Brief that hands off to the parties’ own professionals.

A market is built on it in one direction. First a wiki of what the market knows, written with someone who knows it. Then a twin, filled with synthetic participants, to watch the market behave. Then the marketplace itself.

2 · The solution · A second opinion

Should I take this seriously?

A fair question about any project that describes itself. We asked an AI model to answer it, under a written instruction to be balanced and honest about the strengths and the weaknesses, and we publish the answer with the instruction beside it.

  • Is the thinking behind this reasonable and credible?
  • Does anyone else offer a comparable or better approach, and at what cost?
  • Where does this approach shine, and where should I look for another?

Commissioned by the founder, so not independent. The page gives you the same question to put to any AI model you choose.

3 · Examples · Demos

Can I see it work?

Yes, at more than one stage. Follow one market, skills-based job matching, from its wiki and question page to a twin filled with synthetic participants. Then take a closer look at a second twin, a three-sided precision-machining market.

Some demos are open to everyone and some are by request for now. The page says which, and what in each is real and what is staged. The job-matching example is worked through one specific case, Indigenous workers and the employers who want to hire them, because a specific case is more useful for diagnosis and for learning. It was inspired by casual conversations with people who know the field, not commissioned, and it is built solely from public sources.

4 · Cost

What does a build cost?

The software is free. The cost is people’s time, and most of that is the judgment of someone who knows the market.

For a market of moderate complexity, a new wiki and a first demonstration twin come to roughly 215 to 355 hours of skilled work, and about US$100 to US$1,200 in AI fees and tools.

First estimates, from a small number of builds. The page says how small.

Take part · Contribute

Can I help?

Yes. The frictions that keep markets thin are common across thousands of markets, so the layer that fights them can be built once and reused. DeeperPoint is building that layer as free, open-source software: no licence fee, no usage tier, and no upsell behind the demo. Its four bodies of code are either MIT-licensed or have their source freely available with one condition.

Code, knowledge of a market, a market to test on, and honest critique all have a place to land.
